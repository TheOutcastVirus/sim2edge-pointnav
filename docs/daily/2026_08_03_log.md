# 2026-08-03 — The loss was the problem, and the uncertainty was lying

Three findings, all located by measurement rather than by argument.

**The plateau is the loss, and the estimator was never the binding constraint.**
A consensus vote recovers per-tick ego-motion at 0.96 cm/m of drift where least
squares, on the *identical residuals*, gets 89 cm/m -- so the scan evidence is
extractable after all. But putting it in front of the head made the held-out
mean **worse**, and the arithmetic says why: the vote's per-tick variance is 5.5x
the command's, the training loss weights per-tick accuracy 69x above the
accumulated belief error it stands in for, and per tick the command wins. Four
consecutive encoder improvements were null for one reason, and it is not the
encoder.

**The arrival bound covers 9.5% of the cases it claims to cover 95% of.** The
declared 1-sigma is 8.8× smaller than the true belief error. This is the
mechanism behind declaring arrival at a true 31–34 cm, and it is a model-form
error: a systematic scale error is being propagated as a random walk.

Phase 5 now exists in part: the deployable step exports, reproduces eager
numerics exactly, and costs 0.69 ms of a 50 ms control period.

No closed-loop evaluation ran today, so **no end-to-end number has moved**. The
best closed-loop result is still 75.8% navigation success / 72.7% correct
declared arrival / 11.5 cm mean error, from 2026-08-01.

## 2. The scan vote

### The argument

Yesterday established that the motion is in the scans (median frame difference
tracks travel at ratio ~1.0) and that roughly a third of bearings are gross
outliers at depth edges. Those two facts together are a precise indictment of
*least squares*, not of the data.

A squared residual is unbounded. One outlier at a depth edge contributes more to
the objective than a hundred good bearings, and 30% of them do not add noise —
they relocate the minimum. Least squares has a breakdown point of zero. Both
solvers tried yesterday, point-to-line and full ICP, minimize exactly that, which
is why both returned nothing despite being exact on analytic rooms.

Scoring by *agreement* inverts the failure. For each candidate translation every
bearing contributes

```
exp(-(dr + dx cos theta + dy sin theta)^2 / 2 sigma^2)
```

which is bounded in `[0, 1]`. An outlier's residual is large under every
candidate, so it contributes ~0 to all of them and cannot move the peak — it
abstains rather than votes.

### The measurement

`scripts/probe_scan_vote.py`, on `belief_onpolicy_v15` validation episodes at 1×
sensor noise, 976 per-tick samples, scored against the true displacement:

| estimator | drift | scatter | mean error | corr(x) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| command dead reckoning | 2.10 cm/m | 0.10 cm | 0.08 cm | — |
| **consensus vote** | **0.96 cm/m** | 0.57 cm | 0.44 cm | 0.924 |
| least squares, same residuals | 89.3 cm/m | 11.0 cm | 7.65 cm | 0.084 |
| estimating zero | 100.0 cm/m | 0.73 cm | 1.90 cm | 0.0 |

The least-squares row is the control and it is the important one: it is computed
from the same `difference` and `weight` tensors the vote succeeds on, in the same
loop. It reproduces yesterday's ICP result. The data was never the constraint.

**Read drift, not mean error** -- but read the scatter too. Belief error is an
integral of these residuals, so a biased estimator accumulates linearly while a
zero-mean one accumulates as a random walk. The vote's mean error (0.44 cm) is
*worse* than the command's (0.08 cm) and its drift is less than half, which
reads as a clear win for the vote.

It is not, and §2b works out why: at 220 steps the two effects cancel almost
exactly. Quoting drift alone here would have been the same mistake as quoting
the mean alone, one column over.

### Two things that were easy to get wrong

**Refine the peak locally.** A soft-argmax over the whole candidate grid is
dragged toward the grid centre by whatever pedestal the surface carries. The grid
is centred on zero and the robot is really moving ~2 cm forward, so that
shrinkage appears as a systematic underestimate of travel — precisely the
component that integrates. Windowing the refinement around the hard peak cut
drift from 1.30 to 0.96 cm/m.

**Do not lengthen the baseline.** This looked like free signal: displacement
grows linearly with the frame gap while range noise does not, so SNR should
improve. Measured:

| gap | vote drift |
|---|---:|
| 1 | 0.96 cm/m |
| 2 | 10.8 cm/m |
| 4 | 81.2 cm/m |
| 8 | 87.3 cm/m |

The range-flow model behind the vote is first-order in displacement and compares
equal bearings. Both assumptions fail once the robot has moved far enough to
shift scene content between bearings and to open and close occlusions. Gap 1 is
not a tuning choice, it is the only regime where the model holds. Recorded as a
measured negative so it is not retried.

### What is in the model

`ScanVote` holds **no parameters** and runs under `no_grad`. There is nothing to
learn in it: the projection geometry is exact and the kernel width is the range
noise. It has the same status as `sensors.deskew_history` — fixed sensor
processing that happens to sit inside the model. The learned part is how the head
*reads* the surface, which is where the remaining judgement lives:

* the vote's implied correction to the command, in units of the correction
  limit, so a near-identity weight is the solution;
* the surface itself, through a small `Conv2d` stack, because an isolated peak, a
  ridge and a broad plateau are three different reliabilities sharing one argmax;
* peak sharpness, fed to the confidence gate. This reaches the gate *without*
  passing through a proposal initialized near zero, which is the starvation
  §1 diagnosed, addressed structurally rather than through the loss.

Sharpness is honest about what it measures. It detects how much bearing-diverse
evidence a scan carries, **not** how corrupted it is — 30% outliers move it by
under 2%, because they add a pedestal that normalizing by the peak removes. It
still correlates −0.46 with the vote's error on the corpus, because sparse
returns are the failure that actually occurs.

### One frame error, caught by reading the diff

The vote's estimate and the command it is differenced against both live in the
*newest* frame, because that is the bearing grid the scans were aligned into.
The head's output does not: `translation()` adds it to a dead-reckoned
displacement in the body frame at the **start** of the tick. The residual handed
to the trunk was therefore rotated by up to one tick of yaw, about 5°.

That is ~2 mm against an 11.5 mm correction limit at the full turn rate. Small
per tick, systematic across every turning step, and correlated with exactly the
steps where slip is largest. The network does receive gyro and could in
principle have learned to undo it, but spending capacity relearning a rotation
that is exactly known is what this design explicitly declines to do.

It is pinned by a geometric test: a robot driving straight forward while turning
must produce a purely forward residual. The test also checks itself against the
counterfactual — it undoes the rotation and requires the lateral component that
would have resulted to fall outside the tolerance — because a test that cannot
see the bug it guards is worse than none.

The first `belief_vote_v22` run was discarded at step 780 of 1,955 once this was
found, rather than reported. A measurement of the buggy architecture would not
have answered the question the run exists to answer.

### The result: causality up, mean down — and that is the whole finding

`belief_vote_v22`, 1,955 updates, scored beside every other estimator on the
paired offline evaluator in **one invocation**, so these are comparable:

| checkpoint | final | drift/m | shuffled degradation | conf matched / shuffled |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| v14 `iter_1130` | 8.56 cm | 2.51 | +0.68 cm | 0.133 / 0.083 |
| v19 control | 8.82 cm | 2.59 | +0.49 cm | 0.088 / 0.072 |
| v19 derotated | **8.53 cm** | 2.51 | +0.67 cm | 0.123 / 0.100 |
| v21 gate fix | 8.92 cm | 2.62 | +0.75 cm | 0.958 / 0.198 |
| **v22 vote** | 9.38 cm | 2.76 | **+0.96 cm** | **0.757 / 0.045** |
| dead reckoning | 10.71 cm | 3.15 | — | — |

On the mean the vote is the **worst** of the five. On the number yesterday's log
said to read instead — shuffled-lidar degradation — it is the **best by 40%**,
and the confidence gate finally discriminates: 0.757 on a matched scan against
0.045 on a mismatched one, a 17× separation where v14 managed 1.6×.

So the head is genuinely using the lidar more than any previous checkpoint, and
it is doing *worse*. That is not a contradiction to be explained away. It is the
finding, and it identifies the actual constraint.

## 2b. Why every encoder improvement has been worth nothing

Take the per-tick numbers from the probe seriously as a *variance* statement,
not just a bias one:

| | per-tick bias | per-tick scatter |
|---|---:|---:|
| command dead reckoning | +0.040 cm | 0.102 cm |
| consensus vote | −0.015 cm | 0.566 cm |

Over a 220-step episode, bias accumulates linearly and scatter as its square
root:

| estimator | systematic | random | total |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| command only | 8.77 cm | 1.52 cm | **8.90 cm** |
| vote only | 3.24 cm | 8.40 cm | **9.00 cm** |
| optimal blend, α ≈ 0.5 | 2.81 cm | 4.24 cm | **5.08 cm** |

The vote used per tick is worth **exactly nothing** — its variance consumes
precisely the bias advantage it buys. That is not a training failure; it is
arithmetic, and it predicts v22's result before the run.

**And filtering cannot rescue it.** Averaging the vote over a window cuts its
per-sample variance but introduces correlation that exactly compensates in the
sum, and the belief error is a sum. The integral of N noisy measurements does
not care how they were smoothed on the way in. That rules out the
`persistent_calibration` and `motion_state_size` machinery as the fix, which was
the obvious next thing to try.

### The loss has been asking for the wrong thing

Now the mechanism for four consecutive null results. The total loss is

```
20.0 * translation_loss  (per-tick, smooth_l1, normalized by V_MAX*DT)
 1.0 * belief_loss       (accumulated, squared, normalized by GOAL_TOLERANCE_M)
```

At the operating point those evaluate to about **1.0 and 0.014** — the per-tick
term outweighs the integral it is a proxy for by **69×**. And per tick, the
command is the better estimator by a wide margin: inverse-variance weighting of
a 0.102 cm scatter against a 0.566 cm one gives the scan about **3% of the
weight**.

So gradient descent has been doing exactly the right thing for the objective it
was handed, and the objective is the wrong one. Every encoder improvement made
scan evidence *more available*; none of them changed the fact that using it
costs per-tick accuracy. The head converged to a better command rescaling every
time — which is precisely what yesterday's ablation measured when it found the
head reading `prev_action` 4.6× more than the gyro, without identifying why.

v22 is the confirming case. It is the first checkpoint where the gate has real
dynamic range and the head actually leans on the lidar, and it is the first one
where the mean got materially worse. Forced to use the scan under a per-tick
loss, it does the thing the loss rewards least.

### The integral-weighted loss works, on the axis it was aimed at

`belief_integral_v23` — the same configuration as v22 with `--translation-coef 2
--belief-coef 20`, about 3× in favour of the integral — scored on the same
paired instrument:

| checkpoint | final | drift/m | shuffled degradation | conf matched / shuffled |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| v19 derotated | **8.53 cm** | 2.51 | +0.67 cm | 0.123 / 0.100 |
| v22 vote, per-tick loss | 9.38 cm | 2.76 | +0.96 cm | 0.757 / 0.045 |
| **v23 vote, integral loss** | 8.73 cm | 2.57 | **+1.80 cm** | 0.600 / 0.025 |
| dead reckoning | 10.71 cm | 3.15 | — | — |

Reweighting the loss recovered most of what forcing the vote in had cost (9.38 →
8.73) and **more than doubled lidar causality again** — 1.80 cm against v22's
0.96 and 0.67 for the best checkpoint without the vote. Removing the lidar now
sends the error from 8.73 to 10.53 cm, which is 91% of the way back to dead
reckoning. For the first time in this project the estimator is genuinely
lidar-driven rather than a command rescaling.

The mean still does not beat the best baseline (8.73 against 8.53). §2c is why,
and it is not something more training fixes.

## 2c. How much scan-based ego-motion this sensor actually supports

The `sqrt(S/L)` scaling says the way to cut belief error is a longer baseline:
matching over 40 cm instead of 2.3 cm should buy a factor of four. The per-tick
vote cannot do it, because its range-flow model is first order and assumes
equal-bearing correspondence — but a correlative scan matcher has neither
limitation. It works in Cartesian space against a blurred likelihood field, so
displacement is exact and no bearing correspondence is assumed, while outliers
still abstain under every candidate.

`scripts/probe_correlative_match.py` implements it. Validated on analytic rooms
it recovers 30 cm displacements to **0.3 cm**, including under 1.2 rad of
simultaneous rotation.

On corpus scans it fails completely — 58 cm of error on a 40 cm baseline. So the
question was put to the data directly: **does the true displacement score better
than no displacement at all?**

| baseline | travel | truth outscores zero | median score ratio |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 tick | 2 cm | **97%** of windows | 1.12 |
| 4 ticks | 9 cm | 43% | 0.95 |
| 9 ticks | 21 cm | 41% | 0.91 |
| 19 ticks | 40 cm | 44% | 0.88 |

At one tick the motion is unambiguously present, which is why the vote works. By
four ticks it is gone: a *static* alignment explains the newer scan better than
the true one does, in the majority of windows. This is not an estimator failure
— it is measured on the score surface itself, with no search involved.

That closes the `sqrt(L)` route. The information decays with baseline faster
than the longer lever recovers, so per-tick is not a choice but the only regime
available, and the per-tick variance floor (0.566 cm) therefore bounds what any
frame-to-frame estimator can achieve here. Combined with §2b: a perfectly
weighted per-tick blend lands near 5 cm and that is the ceiling of this approach.

Why the information decays so fast is the open question, and there is a concrete
suspect. The frames are not snapshots: `sweep_scan` stitches each one from five
stored ticks and **quantizes each beam's acquisition time to the nearest tick**,
which its own docstring costs at "half a control period, or 25 ms". At 0.46 m/s
that is 1.15 cm of unmodelled per-beam position error — the same size as the
per-tick signal, and it does not cancel between frames. `LIDAR_SPIN_HZ = 5.5` is
also still unverified against the A1. Both are sensor-reconstruction properties
rather than estimator properties, which is where the next look belongs — along
with local-map accumulation, whose whole purpose is to average per-frame
stitching error away.

The blend arithmetic also sets the ceiling honestly: even a perfectly weighted
combination lands at 5.08 cm, right at the gate's 5 cm threshold. Beating it
requires the vote's *scatter* to come down, not its bias — to ≤0.46 cm for 5 cm
of belief error, and ≤0.27 cm for 3 cm. Grid resolution is not the limit
(scatter is 0.56 cm at 21, 31 and 41 bins alike), so that is the open question.

## 3. The declared uncertainty was not describing the error

On hardware the belief is the only arrival signal there is. The robot stops when
its own upper confidence bound on remaining distance falls inside
`arrival_radius_m`, so a covariance that understates the error does not produce a
cautious robot that stops late — it produces a confident one that stops in the
wrong place and reports success.

`scripts/probe_arrival_honesty.py` on `iter_1130.pt`, 1,383 scored steps:

| quantity | measured |
|---|---:|
| mean belief error | 3.01 cm |
| mean declared 1-sigma | 0.30 cm |
| **error / declared sigma** | **8.76** |
| **coverage at 1.96 sigma** | **9.5%** (nominal 95%) |
| error growth with travel | 3.56 cm/m |
| declared sigma growth with travel | 0.32 cm/m |

The last two rows are the diagnosis. `belief_losses` calibrates the variance head
against *per-step* squared translation error and `_propagate_covariance` then sums
those independently down the episode. That is a random walk, growing as the square
root of step count. The dominant error is a systematic wheel-scale bias, which
grows linearly in distance. The declared bound therefore falls behind the true
error by roughly the square root of the episode length, silently.

No amount of retraining the variance head fixes this. It is the wrong model form.

### The fix, and what it costs

`drift_scale_uncertainty` adds the systematic term, carrying cumulative path
length as one more float of explicit state. The variance increment across a step
is `eps^2 * ((s + ds)^2 - s^2)`; writing it as a difference of squares rather
than `eps^2 * ds^2` is the whole point, since the cross term `2 s ds` is what
makes the error accumulate linearly instead of as a random walk.

| `drift_scale_uncertainty` | error / declared σ | 1.96σ coverage |
|---|---:|---:|
| 0.0 (every existing checkpoint) | 8.76 | 9.5% |
| 0.03 | 1.46 | 78.3% |
| **0.05** | **0.96** | **94.2%** |

0.05 is calibrated for the current estimator.

**Be clear about what an honest bound buys and what it does not.** It widens by
`1.96 * 0.05 * s`, which is 49 cm after five metres — far outside the 15 cm
arrival radius. A calibrated robot therefore *declines to declare arrival* on a
long episode rather than declaring it wrongly. That converts false arrivals into
timeouts, which is the right trade for hardware: a robot that stops and says it
is unsure beats one that confidently stops in the wrong place.

It does not manufacture accuracy. Reaching the 85% correct-arrival gate needs an
estimator that drifts less. That is what §2 is for, and the two changes are
complementary rather than alternatives.

Default is 0.0, so every existing checkpoint keeps its exact behaviour, and a
checkpoint that declares a drift term refuses to run without the carried
distance rather than silently restoring the dishonest bound.

## 4. Phase 5 exists now, in part

`scripts/export_belief.py` exports the deployable step through `torch.export` —
the exact front end `to_edge_transform_and_lower` consumes — and fails if the
exported program does not reproduce the eager module on adversarial state:
non-trivial belief, inflated covariance, a partly elapsed arrival count, and a
scan with 25% missing returns.

On `iter_1130.pt`: **41 distinct operators, 491,159 parameters, parity exactly
0.0, 0.69 ms median host-CPU latency against a 50 ms control period.**

### The vote stays out of the exported graph, and that had to be built

This started as a comment rather than a mechanism, and running the script is
what exposed it. The first version "removed" the vote by setting the module to
`None`, which does not work at all on a checkpoint trained with one: the trunk's
first layer is sized to include the vote's features, so it raises a shape error
rather than degrading. A claim in a docstring that the code does not support is
worse than no claim.

`compute_vote` is now a real entry point and `step_state` takes an optional
precomputed vote, so the network is traced with the vote's outputs as inputs. On
the same checkpoint:

| | operators | host latency |
|---|---:|---:|
| vote outside the graph | 50 | 1.17 ms |
| vote traced in | 56 | 2.46 ms |

The extra six are `gather`, `cumsum`, `flip`, `remainder`, `floor` and a
`set_grad_enabled` higher-order wrapper, none of which the reference models
proved delegated to HTP. It is also an argmax over a consensus surface measuring
centimetres, and INT8 across the candidate grid is about 1 mm; quantizing a
quantity whose whole content is the sharpness of one peak degrades without
erroring.

**The seam is the parameter boundary, not the vote branch.** An earlier version
of the split put the convolution that reads the surface on the preprocessing
side as well, which would have run learned weights in float on the CPU and
quietly spread the model across the quantization boundary. `_vote_surfaces` is
parameter-free geometry; `_encode_vote` has weights and stays in the graph, with
the surface crossing as a small image.

A test requires the precomputed path to be bit-identical to computing the vote
inline. That is the `rollout`-versus-`step` bug class, and a divergence would
appear only as an unexplained sim-to-real gap. The vote itself costs 0.42 ms
single-threaded at 21 bins and 0.86 ms at 31, so the budget is not in question.

Still unbuilt: INT8 calibration from the final policy's own state distribution,
the QNN lowering, the C++ node, and the preprocessing parity test against it.
`archive/v1-numpy-pipeline` has a working version of all four.

## 6. The ceiling is the sensor model, and it is a simulation artifact

Everything above chases an estimator. The estimator was never the constraint.

### The measurement

Take two reconstructed scan frames. Place the older one using the **true**
relative pose — not the command, not an estimate, the ground truth from
`batch.belief`. Then ask how far the best alignment still sits from zero. A
perfect sensor reconstruction would answer zero.

| | 0× sensor noise | 1× sensor noise |
|---|---:|---:|
| baseline 1 tick | **0.665 cm** | 0.629 cm |
| baseline 4 ticks | **7.81 cm** | 7.13 cm |

Two things to notice.

**Sensor noise is irrelevant.** The 0× and 1× columns agree. This is not range
noise; it is the reconstruction disagreeing with itself.

**The 4-tick figure is the sweep period.** `LIDAR_SPIN_HZ = 5.5` gives a 0.182 s
revolution, which is 3.6 control ticks, and the distance travelled in one
revolution at full speed is **8.36 cm**. The measured 4-tick inconsistency is
7.8 cm. Frames more than one sweep period apart are stitched from *disjoint*
sub-scan sets, and their geometric distortions do not cancel.

### Why this ends the argument

The quantity the estimator must measure is the slip residual: **0.078 cm per
tick**. The reconstruction's own frame-to-frame inconsistency is **0.665 cm** at
one tick and **7.8 cm** at four. The noise is 8× the signal at the only baseline
where matching works at all, and 100× at the baseline that would otherwise carry
enough signal.

No estimator can recover a quantity an order of magnitude below the noise floor
of its own input. That single fact explains, without any further hypothesis:

* three architectures plateauing at the same held-out error (2026-08-01);
* a point-to-line solver and a full ICP recovering nothing while being exact on
  analytic rooms (2026-08-02);
* derotation, the scaled difference channel, and the gate fix each being
  correctly diagnosed, correctly implemented, and worth nothing (§1, and
  2026-08-02);
* the consensus vote recovering the slip residual with correlation **0.03**
  while correlating 0.92 with truth — because truth is nearly the command
  already (§2b);
* the observability collapsing between baseline 1 and baseline 4 (§2c) — that is
  the sweep period, not a property of rooms;
* scan-to-map failing too (correlation 0.02), because averaging ten frames
  cannot fix a distortion that is per-frame and systematic.

Every one of those was read as an estimator or encoder problem. None of them
were.

### What it means for the robot, which is the point

`sweep_scan` stitches each frame from `SPIN_TICKS = 5` stored scans and
**quantizes each beam's acquisition time to the nearest stored tick**. Its own
docstring prices that at "half a control period, or 25 ms" — 1.15 cm of
unmodelled per-beam position error at full speed, and the measured 1-tick
inconsistency of 0.665 cm sits squarely inside that. The deskew then compensates
each frame using the *commanded* speed, so two frames are corrected by two
different approximations of the same motion.

**A real RPLIDAR A1 timestamps every return.** The deployed system can deskew
each beam at its true acquisition time against the true elapsed interval; it
never has to quantize to a 50 ms control grid, and it never has to stitch
disjoint sub-scan sets into frames that disagree with each other. The hardware
has strictly better information than this simulator has been supplying.

So the conclusion is not "lidar cannot measure ego-motion on this robot." It is
that **this corpus cannot answer the question**, and every architectural
conclusion drawn from it about scan-based localization is an artifact of the
sensor model rather than a fact about the sensor.

### Order of work, revised

1. **Fix `sensors.py` before touching the model again.** Place each beam at its
   own sub-tick pose instead of rounding to the nearest stored scan, and deskew
   with the true elapsed interval rather than a command-derived approximation.
   The acceptance test is the table above: consecutive frames placed by the true
   pose must align to well under the 0.078 cm/tick signal, and the 4-tick figure
   must stop tracking the sweep period.
2. Recollect. Everything downstream is measured on data that cannot support it.
3. Only then re-run the estimator comparison. The consensus vote, the integral
   loss and the honest covariance are all implemented, tested, and waiting —
   §2b's arithmetic says an optimally weighted blend reaches ~5 cm *at the
   current noise floor*, and the whole point is that the floor is movable.
4. `LIDAR_SPIN_HZ = 5.5` is still unverified against the A1's real
   configuration. It now sets a failure scale that has been mistaken for physics,
   so it is no longer a detail.

## 7. The sub-tick fix: implemented, measured, and it does not work

§6 diagnosed the ceiling as beam-age quantization in `sweep_scan` and prescribed
sub-tick reconstruction. That is now implemented, tested and measured, and the
diagnosis was **wrong**.

### The instrument, corrected first

`scripts/probe_sensor_consistency.py` places one frame into another using the
true relative pose and measures the residual. Its first version searched a fixed
±10 cm, which silently caps the answer once the baseline exceeds ~4 ticks and
then reports the *travel* as if it were reconstruction error. Validated on
analytic rooms, the matcher's own floor with a baseline-scaled search is flat:

| baseline | matcher floor on analytic rooms |
|---|---:|
| 1 tick | 0.21 cm |
| 4 ticks | 0.24 cm |
| 9 ticks | 0.27 cm |

The §6 figures (0.665 / 7.81 cm) came from the capped instrument and a smaller
sample. **Corrected, with the search scaled to the baseline:**

| baseline | nearest-tick (current) | sub-tick (new) |
|---|---:|---:|

Widening the search made the numbers *worse*, not better, which is the right

than the search being unable to reach it.

### What survives, and what does not

**Survives.** The ceiling is real and it is in the reconstruction, not the
estimator. At one tick it is 1.1 cm against a 0.078 cm/tick slip signal — **14×
the quantity any estimator must measure**, and 5× the matcher's own floor. It is
not sensor noise: the 0× and 1× columns agree throughout.

**Does not survive.** Beam-age quantization is not the cause. Sub-tick
reconstruction — interpolating both the range and the motion correction at each
beam's true acquisition time, kept consistent with each other — changes nothing
and is marginally worse. The 25 ms rounding was a real approximation and it was
not the binding one.

The code stays, off by default, tested, and honestly labelled. It is correct and
it is cheap; it simply is not the constraint. That is the same disposition
`--derotate-scan-history` earned on 2026-08-02, for the same reason: fixing a
defect that is not the bottleneck buys nothing, and recording the measurement is
what stops it being retried.

### The next candidate, with its arithmetic

`rasterize_lidar` snaps every deskewed return back onto a fixed 720-bin angular
grid. Deskew moves beams to irregular bearings; the grid quantizes them again.
At the corpus's median valid range of 1.23 m, one 0.5° bin subtends 1.08 cm of
arc, so uniform quantization contributes **0.311 cm RMS** — about a third of the
measured 1.095 cm floor, and on its own already 4× the slip signal.

That is a *representation* fix rather than a sensor fix, and it is the cheaper
of the two: keep the deskewed `(range, exact bearing)` pairs instead of
re-binning them onto a grid. The estimator's Cartesian point cloud never needed
the grid; only the actor's 180-bin observation does.

It accounts for a third, not all, so it is a candidate and not a diagnosis. The
remaining two thirds are unexplained, and the honest list of what has been ruled
out is now: sensor noise (measured), beam-age quantization (measured), and
search failure in the instrument (measured).

### Status

Three hypotheses, one confirmed measurement, two refuted causes, and no
end-to-end number moved. The durable artifact is
`probe_sensor_consistency.py`: before today the sensor model's fitness for
ego-motion estimation was not measurable, and every architectural conclusion in
Phase 4 was drawn without it. It now has an acceptance bar — frame inconsistency
below 0.078 cm/tick — that no configuration has met.
